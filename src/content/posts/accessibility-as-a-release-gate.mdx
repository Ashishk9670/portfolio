When I joined Triomics, accessibility was reviewed when a report was due — roughly twice a year. That cadence
has a predictable failure mode: by the time anyone looks, six months of features have shipped, the issues are
baked into components other screens depend on, and every fix competes with roadmap work. A checklist that only
gets opened twice a year isn't a practice. It's a compliance exercise nobody remembers until it's due.

So I proposed two changes, and we made both stick:

1. **A mandatory accessibility checklist before any new feature ships to production.**
2. **A monthly accessibility review across every product shipped that month**, replacing the twice-yearly report.

The first moves the check to the cheapest point to fix things. The second makes sure nothing waits half a year
to be looked at.

## What an audit actually runs

The automated half is Playwright walking every page and every form, with axe-core scanning each view against
WCAG 2.0, 2.1, and 2.2 A/AA rules plus best practices. Findings are grouped by WCAG guideline and by impact —
critical, serious, moderate, minor — and roll up into VPAT-style conformance reports.

But axe on its own leaves big gaps, so the suite carries around 25 custom checks for what a rule engine can't
see: keyboard navigation and keyboard traps, focus order, focus not obscured (2.4.11), on-focus and on-input
behavior, text resize and text spacing, non-text contrast, use of color, content on hover or focus, reading
order, language of parts, link purpose, multiple ways, pointer cancellation, dragging movements, accessible
authentication (3.3.8), and status messages.

Forms and sign-in get their own dedicated audit alongside the all-pages pass. That's where an accessibility
blocker doesn't just inconvenience someone — it stops them outright.

## Where automated scans stop

This is the part that matters most, and the part a passing scan hides:

- **axe-core automates only one rule specific to WCAG 2.2** — target size. The rest of 2.2's new criteria need
  custom checks or a person.
- **A scan confirms presence, not meaning.** It can tell you alt text exists, not that it's useful; that a label
  is present, not that it makes sense; that focus is visible, not that the order is logical.
- **Some heuristics are low-confidence.** Every one of those findings needs a human call before it becomes a
  ticket, or the backlog fills with noise and people stop trusting it.
- **Some criteria are manual on purpose.** Error prevention (3.3.4) and redundant entry (3.3.7) are tested by
  hand, because automating them would change shared QA data other people depend on.
- **Media, timing, and gesture criteria** are checked manually too.

## What manual and exploratory testing add

The automated suite tells you what's broken against a rule. The manual pass answers a different question:
can someone actually complete this? That means keyboard-only passes through critical journeys end to end, the
way a keyboard user really moves through the product; exploratory sessions on new features; and a manual
checklist for everything a rule engine can't judge — meaning, context, and whether the experience holds
together.

## The lesson

The tooling was never the hard part. Moving the check to before production, and making the review a monthly
habit instead of a report deadline, is what changed the outcome. Automation makes that habit cheap enough to
sustain; it doesn't replace the judgment.
